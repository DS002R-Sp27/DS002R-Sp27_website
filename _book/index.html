Course Description:

Data science has rapidly gained recognition within the social and environmental sciences because it offers powerful new ways to ask questions. Using the R programming language, we will learn data gathering and cleaning, visualization, network analysis, modelling, visualization, ethics, and interpretation. We will explore how a range of computational methods can be used to garner new meanings from sources such as weather monitors, press releases, websites, government programs, and more. Class sessions will be taught interactively using collaborative coding tools . Students will follow along with workshop-style lectures by using and modifying code in real time to analyze data and visualize results. The course will cover such topics as the South African water crisis, Hurricane Katrina, the California Wildfires, and water rights along the Colorado River. The class will culminate with final projects in which students implement the full data science pipeline, from idea generation to results and interpretations, using a topic and data of their choosing.

Who is this course for? This course is primarily designed for people with some prior exposure to programming (such as DS1). While this course may be useful for programmers and computer scientists seeking social applications for their skills, the materials will mostly be introductory. This course is also for students who seek to develop a project related to data science and society. Over the course of sixteen weeks, students will have opportunities to pose questions and ideas, receive feedback, and collaborate with others on building out a complete research project.

Learning Goals: At the end of the course, students will have experience using data science tools to address questions related to society and the environment. We will learn what questions can be addressed by data science and consider how we can effectively convey this information with the tools that we have. We will also think critically about where data come from, how they are represented, and our role as data scientists in the twenty-first century.

Classroom policies and format: Please arrive on time and having completed the assigned materials. Being prepared to discuss the readings/videos/audio will enrich the learning experience of the entire class. Completing the assigned materials by the beginning of each week will also allow students to identify areas of interest and consider possible research questions for problem sets or for the final project.

The general class structure will be as follows:

  • Warm Up Every day, we’ll do some sort of warm-up related to the course material. This will often involve drawing and modelling data by hand.

  • Theory We will discuss new concepts and ideas. This part of our class will be technology free! Why? Data science has existed long before the modern era of computing capabilities. To refine their methodological approach, researchers in the modern age often practice on small data. So we will do the same.

  • Laboratory To implement theories of data science, students will participate in coding exercises. Drawing from what we learned, we will take out our laptops and devices, and try to do some (modern) data analysis ourselves using R. As with learning to code in any language, you can expect some amount of roadbumps/frustration. But we will work through this together!

Outside of class, you can expect to do the following:

  • Reading Course Materials On your own time, your should be keeping up with course readings, notes, and other materials. These will help solidify concepts and may provide ideas for projects. I expect you to spend roughly 4 hours per week with the course materials.

  • Independent Project/Problem Set Work On your own time, you will have a chance to implement the full data science pipeline (from ideation and data gathering to modelling and interpretation). I expect projects and assignments to take roughly 4 hours per week, although this may vary across the semester (don’t put it all off until the end!).

Textbooks We will mostly use two textbooks for this course. The good news? Both are free, and online! (Though you are welcome to purchase either or both if you wish). For the rest of the Syllabus, I’ll refer to these by their titles alone.

Matthew J. Salganik. 2017. Princeton University Press. Bit By Bit: Social Research in the Digital Age. This is probably the most complete book out there on doing data science for sociology (and other social sciences). The sections on data generation and research ethics are especially insightful, in my opinion.

Healy, Kieran. 2018. Princeton University Press. Data Visualization: A Practical Introduction. This is an excellent guide to working with data. It is complete with exercises and data to use in R, and it provides a welcome reminder to think seriously about the aesthetic elements of data science.

Software This class will primarily be taught in R, which is free, open access, built for and by data scientists, and has many wonderful and current packages for social scientists. You can download R here. We will use R Studio as an interface, which can be downloaded for free here. This being said, programming languages evolve and sometimes become defunct. The theoretical concepts that we cover in this class should be applicable to a range of data science programming tools. If you wish to use an alternative software, such as Python or Julia, or an alternative interface, such as Jupyter Notebook, I completely encourage this!

Participation

Coming to class prepared (having done any readings or assignments) is vital for participation. In class, students can participate in many ways. First, students can participate in classroom discussions and activities. Second, students can complete in-class coding exercises. Engaging in class activities, working well with peers and in groups, and asking relevant questions during class are all valid forms of participation. At some point in the semester, everyone will be required to share a brief visualization from their final project work.

Problem Sets

There will be seven problem sets over the course of the semester. Students are expected to complete each of these on time and turn in assignments on Canvas. Most of the assignments should be turned in as .PDF files generated by R Markdown or an alternative. Students can fork assignments and update their progress using GitHub (which serves as a useful tool for collaboration and a way to assess effort). The assignments involve synthesizing, exploring, and connecting class concepts, and should reflect student’s own ideas.

Final Project, Proposal, and Presentation

The final project is intended to be an opportunity for students to apply some of the skills that we learn in this class toward a pressing and important issue. Much of this class is very introductory - we will go broad, but not deep - however, in the final project you can go deeper into one specific method or topic area.

Students can work in groups of up to four for the final projects. Individual projects are also fine - but I encourage you to collaborate if you have the capacity! Much of the best science is now done in teams, so this type of work is an important skill to practice. As is the norm in science, teams should write details on specifically who contributed what to the project.

The semester goes by quickly, so students should begin thinking about potential final projects early. The project proposal is due in week 4, leaving 12 weeks for students to complete the project. The project proposal should include a potential research question, data source, and method. It is likely that you will want to use one or more methods that we haven’t covered yet in week 4 - this is ok! Provide as much detail as you can in your proposal.

A note on Generative AI: The latest versions of some generative AI software have capabilities to provide human-like intuition and reasoning, and to answer novel problems in interesting ways. These tools might supplement our learning, but they should never replace our learning.

For in class assignments, we will learn most concepts and coding ourselves, without the help of AI. Early research suggests that it can be beneficial for higher-level tasks, but usage might limit learning of lower-level concepts. For example, one study finds that “Although entry-level developers used generative AI the most, it did not appear to benefit them.” Another study notes that AI can reduce skill acquisition in math learning. For these reasons, I believe there are substantial benefits to focusing on conceptual learning, especially at the introductory level.

For out of class assignments, this class requires a lot of coding, problem-posing, and data interpreting. I want you to be able to do data science in the real world. We might want to ask a question like, “how do you read a csv into R?” or “how can I add spaces to my R markdown document?” from time to time. In some cases, AI can be very helpful in answering these questions, often pulling answers from sources like stackoverflow (where humans voluntarily help each other solve problems!). We will also cover coding with agents and other types of AI usage.

For each problem set, students will upload three files: (1) a markdown file, (2) a pdf, and (3) a link to their github file showing edits over time. With each of these, the teaching team will evaluate not only the finished assignment, but how the assignment was revised and changed over time. We will reward - and provide feedback on - your effort.
